## What does "as the crow flies" mean?

**As the crow flies** describes a distance measured in a straight line from one place to another, as if you could fly directly there. It sets aside the roads, bends, bridges and detours a real journey would need.

If someone says their village is "eight miles from the city as the crow flies", they mean the straight line distance is eight miles. The drive could easily be ten or twelve, because the road has to go around hills, fields and water that a bird would simply cross.

The phrase works as an adverb after a distance ("20 miles as the crow flies") and, with hyphens, as an adjective before a noun ("the as-the-crow-flies distance"). In maps and geography the same idea is called **straight line distance** or, over long distances on the curved Earth, **great circle distance**.

## Examples of "as the crow flies" in a sentence

- "The cabin is only three miles from town as the crow flies, but the road winds around the lake for nine."
- "As the crow flies, London is closer to Paris than to Edinburgh."
- "We live 40 miles apart as the crow flies, so meeting halfway is an easy drive for both of us."
- "The new airport is 12 kilometers from the center as the crow flies."
- "The listing said the house was a mile from the beach, but that was as the crow flies; the walk is closer to two."

The second example is true: London to Paris is about 214 miles in a straight line, while London to Edinburgh is about 332 miles. It is the kind of surprise the phrase is good at revealing.

## Where does the phrase come from?

The idea behind the phrase is simple. A bird in flight does not have to follow roads or go around obstacles on the ground, so its path is the natural picture of a direct route. The crow was a familiar bird across Britain and Ireland, which may be why it was the one chosen.

<PhraseTimeline />

The earliest example word historians have found is from 1761, in a Dublin magazine that described a territory in North America as hundreds of miles long and broad "as the crow flies". A 1767 London literary review used the phrase in a way that made its meaning explicit, and it was in regular use by the end of the century. Charles Dickens used it in *Oliver Twist* in 1838, which helped make it familiar to readers everywhere English was read.

A similar Scottish expression, **the crow road**, meaning the most direct way, appears in writing around the same period.

### The sailors' crows story

A popular story says the phrase comes from ships that carried caged crows. Lost at sea, the crew would release a crow, which would fly straight toward the nearest land, and the ship would follow. Some versions add that this is why the lookout platform on a mast is called the crow's nest.

It is an appealing tale, but the evidence does not support it. The earliest uses of "as the crow flies" all describe distances over land, not at sea, and the crow's nest is thought to be named for its shape and position rather than for keeping birds. Releasing birds to find land is an ancient idea, but it is not where this phrase comes from.

## Do crows really fly in a straight line?

Not always. Crows forage, circle, ride the wind and stop to investigate anything interesting, so a crow going about its day rarely flies in a perfect line. When crows commute between a feeding area and their evening roost, though, they often fly fairly directly, and those steady lines across the sky at dusk are a familiar sight.

They are not fast fliers either: the Pennsylvania Game Commission puts the American crow's cruising speed at [25 to 30 mph](https://www.pa.gov/agencies/pgc/wildlife/discover-pa-wildlife/crows).

The phrase is not a claim about bird behavior. It is shorthand for an imaginary perfect line, the same way "a stone's throw" stands for a short distance without anyone measuring a throw.

## Similar phrases in English and other languages

English has a few close relatives of the phrase:

- **Beeline.** "Make a beeline for" means go straight toward something, from the idea that a bee flies straight back to its hive with nectar.
- **As the bird flies.** A less common variant that some people use instead.
- **In a straight line, or point to point.** The plain way to say it.

Many languages have their own expressions for straight line distance, and several of them use a bird too.

## How to use the phrase correctly

- **Put it after the distance.** "Five miles as the crow flies" is the usual order. "As the crow flies, it's five miles" also works.
- **Hyphenate it before a noun.** "An as-the-crow-flies distance of five miles." Many writers rephrase to avoid the hyphens: "a straight line distance of five miles".
- **Don't use it for travel time.** "Twenty minutes as the crow flies" mixes a straight line with a journey; say "about 20 minutes away" instead.
- **It applies to any unit.** Kilometers, miles and nautical miles all work: "30 km as the crow flies".

## How to measure distance as the crow flies

You can measure a straight line distance in three ways:

1. **Use a calculator.** Enter two places in the [crow flies distance calculator](/) at the top of this page and it gives the distance on the curved Earth, with the direction and a map.
2. **Use a mapping app.** Google Maps has a Measure distance tool that draws a straight line between points you choose. Our guide to [measuring as the crow flies on Google Maps](/google-maps-as-the-crow-flies/) walks through it on a computer and a phone.
3. **Use a paper map.** Lay a ruler between the two places and convert with the map's scale. Over long distances this is less exact, because a flat map cannot show the Earth's curve without stretching it.
